## What the second interview adds

The second transcript is useful because its explanatory vocabulary crosses
several scientific levels in a few sentences:

1. Euler is described as carrying the main growth mechanism while viscosity
   adds a further obstacle that the singular behavior must overcome.
2. A collapsing vortex profile is explicitly distinguished from a solution
   of the stated Navier–Stokes problem.
3. In the forced setting, the mismatch can be assigned to a forcing term;
   this does not make the profile an unforced solution.
4. The central task is described as correcting the non-solution without
   importing an inadmissible infinite force.
5. Convex integration and the Euler growth mechanism are described as distinct
   ingredients combined in that correction architecture.
6. The resulting proof and its public explanation remain separate objects:
   identifying an idea or mechanism does not by itself supply a readable,
   checked proof.

analysis, numerical analysis and philosophy of modelling already provide rich

different roles into words such as `mechanism`, `error`, `fix`, `architecture`
and `solution`. NEXAH may be useful here as an **audit and translation
grammar** that keeps these roles apart while leaving all mathematical
definitions and proof authority with the field.

## Keep three limits separate

1. **Analytic blow-up / loss of regularity:** property of a precisely specified PDE solution.
2. **Physical model boundary:** the continuum description no longer adequately represents the physical system.
3. **Numerical failure:** discretization, resolution, conditioning or algorithmic failure prevents reliable computation.

They may be evidentially related, but none is identical to another. Simulation failure is not proof of blow-up; mathematical singularity is not a molecular account of a real fluid.
